# The survey: seven questions, ten real answers

Read this in Stage 5. This is the slowest stage and the only one that turns a guess into
a plan. It takes days. Rushing it is how a Blueprint becomes fiction with a nice cover.

## The instrument

**Six short questions and one very short paragraph question.** Seven in total, on a
Google Form, with the answers going into its linked Google Sheet.

Seven because a form that takes over two minutes gets abandoned by exactly the strangers
whose answers matter most. The friends finish it. That is the wrong sample.

## The bar for the data

**Ten relevant responses from people who are not friends and not family.**

The number is small on purpose. Ten real strangers is achievable in a week and is enough
to kill an obviously wrong assumption. The word that carries the weight is not "ten", it
is "not friends". Friends answer to be kind, and kind answers are what let a wrong plan
survive for a year.

Relevant means the person could actually be the customer from question 2. Ten answers
from ten people who would never buy is zero answers.

## Writing the seven

Each question comes from their own v1, and each one exists to settle one thing. Write the
question, then the line under it that says what it settles:

```text
Q3. In the last month, how did you book your last salon appointment?
    Settles: [Assumption, "they book on WhatsApp"]
```

A question that cannot name what it settles gets cut. That is how seven stays seven.

Cover, in this order of value:

1. The problem in question 4: did it actually happen to them, recently
2. What they did about it: the current workaround, which is the real competitor
3. Money: what they paid, or pay now, for the workaround
4. The customer in question 2: one question that confirms this person is that person
5. The biggest [PENDING] from question 10
6. One more from the two gaps the attack ranked highest
7. The paragraph: open, in their words

## The rules that make answers worth having

**Ask what people did, not what they would do.** "Would you use an app that books
appointments?" gets a yes from everyone and means nothing. "How did you book your last
appointment?" gets the truth. Past behaviour is evidence. Future intention is politeness.

**Never teach the answer.** "Would you like a faster way to book?" contains its own
answer. So does any question with "easily", "better", "frustrating" or "problem" in it.
Describe nothing, ask what happened.

**No question about the product.** The product does not exist. Questions about it collect
imagination.

**The paragraph question stays open and short.** Something like "what is the most
annoying part of <the thing>, in your words". This is where the customer's own language
comes from, and that language is worth more than the six short answers put together. It
goes into the landing page, the ad and the offer later, word for word.

**One answer per question.** No question with "and" in it.

## What the agent does and does not do

Does: write the seven questions, say what each settles, and give the two steps.
Does not: create the Form, send it to anyone, collect anything, or invent a response.

The two steps, in plain words: make a new Google Form and paste the seven questions in;
in the Responses tab, link it to a new Google Sheet. Then share the link where the real
people are, which is a place they named in question 3.

Never suggest sending it to their contact list. That is the friends problem, wearing a
different hat.

## Reading the answers

Count first, judge second. How many responses, how many relevant, how many from
strangers. Say those three numbers before any interpretation.

Then look for the one thing nobody expected. A survey where every answer confirms v1 is
either a friendly sample or a set of teaching questions, and both mean the data is not
usable yet. Say which one you think it is, and why.

Quote, do not summarise, when the paragraph answers say something the plan does not.
Three real sentences from three strangers change a founder's mind. A summary of them
does not.

## Then

v2, the Blueprint, in Stage 6, where every change from v1 carries the response that
caused it, and the go, pivot or tweak sentence is written out loud.
