# The attack

Read this in Stage 3, after the person says yes to it. This stage is deliberately harsh
and they choose it. Nobody gets attacked by surprise.

## Why it exists

A plan written alone gets better looking every time it is read. The gaps do not close,
the reader just gets used to them. The attack is the first outside pressure the plan
meets, and it costs nothing. The second is the survey, and that one costs weeks.

## The shape

Ten areas, one for each question. For each one:

```text
<area>  <score>/10
Gap:    [what is missing or weak, plain words, one or two lines]
Size:   [big / medium / small, and what it costs if it stays]
Lift:   [the one thing that raises this score, doable by them]
```

Then, and only then:

```text
The two that matter most: [two areas, and why those two and not the others]
Three questions worth answering next: [three, answerable by them, one ask each]
```

Nothing else. No summary, no encouragement, no rewrite.

## What the score means, and what it does not

The number is a judgment, not a measurement. Two honest people would score the same plan
differently. So the number is never the point: **the gap named under it is the point.**
That is why a score with no gap is not allowed. If you cannot name what is missing,
write [PENDING] where the score would be and say why.

The one hard rule that keeps scores honest: **nothing supported only by an Assumption
scores above 7.** Not because the idea is bad, but because confidence without evidence is
exactly the failure being caught. A plan can be brilliant and still score 4 on evidence.
Say that out loud when it happens, in one line, so the person does not read a low score
as a verdict on the idea.

Most first plans score low on questions 2, 4, 6 and 9. That is normal and worth saying
once, because a beginner reading ten low scores can quit for the wrong reason.

## Ten ways to be useless, and what to do instead

- "Great start, but..." Cut it. They asked to be told.
- Rewriting their sentence to be better. Not your plan.
- Ten equal problems. Rank, or the person fixes the easy ones.
- A gap they cannot act on this month. Name a lift they can reach.
- Attacking the industry ("salons are a hard business"). Attack this plan.
- Inventing a competitor, a market size, or a statistic to argue with. Never.
- Comparing them to a famous company. It teaches nothing.
- Softening a big gap because they seem attached to it. That attachment is the reason
  to say it plainly.
- Scoring the writing instead of the thinking. A rough sentence about a real customer
  beats a polished sentence about a category.
- Three questions that need research to answer. They must be answerable from memory or
  from one phone call.

## What the attack is not allowed to conclude

That the idea is bad. That is not a finding, it is an opinion, and it is not this
skill's job. The findings are gaps and their sizes. The person decides what that means.

If every area scores low and the honest read is that the plan has no customer yet, say
that as a fact about the plan, in one line: "nine of ten areas rest on Assumptions, so
this is an idea, not yet a plan. That is where the survey starts." No verdict, no advice
to stop.

## After the attack

The person answers what they can. Then v1 is written, with each change traced to the
finding that caused it. Changes with no finding behind them are the agent's opinions
leaking in, and they get removed.

Do not run the attack twice on the same version. Attacking v1 is a real thing to do, but
only after the person has answered; and the interesting comparison is then the scores
side by side, which is why v1 keeps the old ones.
